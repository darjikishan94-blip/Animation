11.18 Explain the following. (a) Why is the tungsten used almost exclusively for filament of electric lamps? (b) Why are the conductors of electric heating devices, such as bread-toasters and electric irons, made of an alloy rather than a pure metal? (c) Why is the series arrangement not used for domestic circuits? (d) How does the resistance of a wire vary with its area of cross-section? (e) Why are copper and aluminium wires usually employed for electricity transmission?

Given

  • Five situations.

Asked

Explanations.

Why there is no animation

  • This question asks for short explanations; the page shows the question and the answers.

Answer

  1. (a) Tungsten has a very high melting point (about 3380 °C), so it can glow white-hot without melting; it also has a fairly high resistivity.
  2. (b) Alloys have higher resistivity than pure metals and do not oxidise (burn) readily at high temperatures.
  3. (c) In series the current is the same in all appliances and the voltage is shared; switching off or failure of one breaks the whole circuit, and appliances cannot get the full 220 V or work independently.
  4. (d) R = ρl/A: resistance is inversely proportional to the area of cross-section.
  5. (e) They have very low resistivity (they are good conductors), so less energy is lost as heat; they are also comparatively cheap (and aluminium is light).

See (a)–(e).
