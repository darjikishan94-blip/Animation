10.15 Given below are observations on molar specific heats at room temperature of some common gases. Hydrogen 4.87, Nitrogen 4.97, Oxygen 5.02, Nitric oxide 4.99, Carbon monoxide 5.01, Chlorine 6.17 (Cv, cal mol–1 K–1). The measured molar specific heats of these gases are markedly different from those for monatomic gases. Typically, molar specific heat of a monatomic gas is 2.92 cal/mol K. Explain this difference. What can you infer from the somewhat larger (than the rest) value for chlorine ?

Given

  • Cv of diatomic gases ≈ 5 cal mol−1 K−1; monatomic ≈ 2.92; chlorine 6.17.

Asked

Explain the difference; what chlorine’s value shows.

Why there is no animation

  • This question asks for explanations rather than describing one physical situation to follow; the page shows the question and a worked answer.

Answer

  1. A monatomic gas stores energy only in 3 translational motions: Cv = (3/2)R ≈ 2.98 cal mol−1 K−1.
  2. A diatomic molecule can also rotate about two axes: Cv = (5/2)R ≈ 4.97 cal mol−1 K−1, matching the table.
  3. Chlorine’s larger value shows that its vibrational motion is also excited at room temperature (it is a heavier molecule with a weaker bond).

Rotation adds R for diatomic gases; chlorine also has vibration excited.
