Q11 A 10.0 kg block is moving on horizontal floor with negligible friction. As shown in the Fig. 7.37, a variable force is applied on the block in its direction of motion from its position at 0 m till 4 m. If the block had a kinetic energy of 180 J when it was at 0 m, find the block’s speed (i) at 0 m, and (ii) at 4 m. Does the block have negative acceleration in any portion of its motion?

Given

  • Mass of the block = 10.0 kg; negligible friction.
  • Fig. 7.37: the force rises from 0 to 50 N between 0 and 1 m, stays at 50 N from 1 m to 3 m, and falls back to 0 between 3 m and 4 m. It acts in the direction of motion.
  • Kinetic energy at 0 m = 180 J.

Asked

The block’s speed (i) at 0 m and (ii) at 4 m, and whether it has negative acceleration anywhere.

How the animation is drawn

  • The graph is drawn under the floor with the same horizontal scale, so the dot on the graph is always directly below the block.
  • The motion is worked out from energy at every point (KE = 180 J + work done so far) and shown 10 times slower than real life.
  • The shaded area is the work done so far. The force, velocity and acceleration arrows each keep one scale throughout.

Answer

  1. (i) 180 = ½ × 10 × v2, so v2 = 36 and v = 6 m/s.
  2. Work done by the force = area under the graph = (½ × 1 × 50) + (50 × 2) + (½ × 1 × 50) = 25 + 100 + 25 = 150 J.
  3. (ii) KE at 4 m = 180 + 150 = 330 J = ½ × 10 × v2, so v2 = 66 and v ≈ 8.1 m/s.
  4. The force never acts backward, so a = F/m is never negative. The acceleration gets smaller between 3 m and 4 m and is zero only at 4 m.

(i) 6 m/s (ii) about 8.1 m/s. No portion of the motion has negative acceleration.

Q12 The gravitational attraction on the surface of the Moon (lunar surface) is about 1/6th of that on the surface of the Earth. An astronaut can throw a ball up to a height of 8 m from the surface of the Earth. How far up will the ball thrown with the same upward velocity travel from the surface of the Moon?

Given

  • Gravity on the Moon ≈ 1/6 of gravity on Earth.
  • On Earth the ball rises to 8 m.
  • The ball is thrown up with the same velocity on the Moon.

Asked

How high the ball goes on the Moon.

How the animation is drawn

  • Heights are true to size, with grid lines every 8 m. Air resistance is neglected (the Moon has no air).
  • The question gives no value of g. The animation uses g = 9.8 m s−2 for Earth so it runs in real time; the heights do not depend on this choice.
  • Both balls are thrown with the same speed u. The weight arrows are to scale (the Moon’s is 1/6 of Earth’s), and faint circles show each ball every 0.5 s.
  • The heights on the grid, other than 8 m, are labelled only when the answers are shown.

Answer

  1. At the highest point all the kinetic energy has become potential energy: ½mu2 = mgh, so h = u2/(2g).
  2. u is the same on Earth and on the Moon, so h ∝ 1/g: gravity 6 times weaker gives a height 6 times greater.
  3. hMoon = 6 × hEarth = 6 × 8 m = 48 m.

The ball rises to about 48 m on the Moon.

Q13 A 1000 kg car is moving along a road at a constant speed. Suddenly, the driver notices some obstruction ahead and applies the brakes to come to a complete stop. The graphical representation of motion of the car starting from the instant the driver spots the traffic ahead is shown in Fig. 7.38. (i) Describe how the car moves between positions A and B. (ii) Calculate the kinetic energy of the car at A. (iii) State the work done by the brakes in bringing the car to a halt between B and C. (iv) What does the kinetic energy of the car transform into?

Given

  • Mass of the car = 1000 kg.
  • Fig. 7.38: speed 35 m/s from A (0 s) to B (1 s); it then falls steadily to 0 at C (3 s).

Asked

(i) How the car moves between A and B. (ii) Its kinetic energy at A. (iii) The work done by the brakes between B and C. (iv) What the kinetic energy turns into.

How the animation is drawn

  • Distances along the road are to scale: A to B and B to C are each 35 m (the areas under the graph). The car is drawn roughly to scale.
  • The position of the obstruction is not given; it is drawn just beyond C.
  • The motion runs at half speed. The braking force is shown only while the car slows down; the glow at the wheels stands for heat.

Answer

  1. (i) From A to B the speed stays at 35 m/s: the car moves uniformly, with no acceleration, covering 35 × 1 = 35 m. (This is the time before the brakes take effect.)
  2. (ii) KE at A = ½mv2 = ½ × 1000 × 352 = 6,12,500 J (6.125 × 105 J).
  3. (iii) Work done by the brakes = final KE − initial KE = 0 − 6,12,500 J = −6,12,500 J. It is negative because the braking force acts opposite to the motion.
  4. (iv) Mainly into heat in the brakes, tyres and road (through friction), with a little sound.

(i) Uniform motion at 35 m/s (ii) 6,12,500 J (iii) −6,12,500 J (iv) heat, and a little sound.

Q14 The potential energy-displacement graph of a 0.5 kg ball moving along a frictionless track is shown in Fig. 7.39. At O, the velocity of the ball is 0 m s−1 and potential energy is 30 J. Calculate the velocity of the ball at P, Q and R.

Given

  • Mass of the ball = 0.5 kg; the track is frictionless.
  • Fig. 7.39: at O the ball is at rest and its potential energy is 30 J. The potential energy is 20 J at P, 30 J at Q and 40 J at R.

Asked

The velocity of the ball at P, Q and R.

How the animation is drawn

  • The track has the same shape as the potential-energy graph, read off Fig. 7.39 (the higher the track, the greater the PE).
  • O is at the top of a small hump, so the ball is given a tiny nudge to the right; this does not change its 30 J.
  • The figure’s displacement axis has no scale, so the timing is illustrative; the speed at every point comes from energy. The ball is treated as sliding, without rolling.

Answer

  1. The track is frictionless, so the total energy stays 30 J (0 KE + 30 J PE at O). At any point KE = 30 J − PE and v = √(2KE/m).
  2. At P: KE = 30 − 20 = 10 J, so v = √(2 × 10 / 0.5) = √40 ≈ 6.3 m/s.
  3. At Q: KE = 30 − 30 = 0, so v = 0. The ball stops for an instant and turns back.
  4. At R: the PE would be 40 J, more than the 30 J the ball has. KE cannot be negative, so the ball never reaches R and has no velocity there.

P: about 6.3 m/s. Q: 0 m/s. R: the ball never gets there.

Q15 A coconut of mass 1.5 kg falls from the top of a coconut tree onto the wet sand on a beach. The height of the tree is 10 m. On impact, the coconut comes to rest by making a depression in the sand. (i) Calculate the velocity of the coconut just before it hits the sand. (ii) Assume that the average resistive force of sand is 3000 N and all of the coconut’s energy is used to create the depression in the sand. Calculate the depth of the depression the coconut makes in the sand. Assume g = 10 m s−2.

Given

  • Mass of the coconut m = 1.5 kg; it falls from rest.
  • Height of the tree h = 10 m; g = 10 m s−2.
  • Average resistive force of the sand F = 3000 N.
  • All the coconut’s energy is used to create the depression.

Asked

(i) The velocity of the coconut just before it hits the sand. (ii) The depth of the depression.

How the animation is drawn

  • Heights in the main picture are true to size. The coconut’s size is not given, so it is drawn larger; the close-up is magnified, and its scale bar appears with the answers.
  • Air resistance is neglected. The fall runs at half speed; the stop in the sand, which takes about 7 milliseconds, runs 400 times slower.
  • The sand’s force is drawn as constant, equal to the average force given.
  • As the question says, all of the coconut’s energy goes into the depression. The small extra work done by its weight while it sinks (about 0.75 J) is ignored.

Answer

  1. (i) Falling from rest, mgh = ½mv2, so v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 10) = √200 ≈ 14.1 m/s.
  2. Energy at impact = mgh = 1.5 × 10 × 10 = 150 J, all of it kinetic.
  3. (ii) The sand takes away all 150 J by doing work against the coconut: F × d = 150 J, so 3000 × d = 150 and d = 0.05 m.

(i) v ≈ 14.1 m/s (ii) The depression is 0.05 m = 5 cm deep.
