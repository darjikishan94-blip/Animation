Q5 A student is slowly lifted straight up in an elevator from the ground level to the top floor of a building. Later, the same student climbs the staircase, all the way to the top. Given that the height of the building is h = 72.5 m, acceleration due to gravity is g = 10 m s−2, and student’s mass is m = 50 kg. (i) Find the gain in the potential energy if the student is lifted straight up to the top. (ii) Find the gain in the potential energy when the student climbs the stairs to the same top. (iii) What do you conclude about the dependence of the potential energy on the path taken?

Given

  • Mass of the student m = 50 kg.
  • Height of the building h = 72.5 m.
  • g = 10 m s−2.
  • Lifted slowly in the elevator; later climbs the stairs to the same top.

Asked

(i) The gain in potential energy in the elevator. (ii) The gain in potential energy on the stairs. (iii) How potential energy depends on the path taken.

How the animation is drawn

  • The height scale is true to size. The student and the elevator are drawn larger than scale so they can be seen.
  • The number of flights of stairs is not given; ten are drawn. Only the vertical rise matters.
  • “Slowly” is drawn as a slow, steady speed, so the student’s kinetic energy stays negligible.
  • The two potential-energy bars share one scale.

Answer

  1. (i) Gain in PE = mgh = 50 × 10 × 72.5 = 36,250 J.
  2. (ii) The student starts on the ground and ends on the same top floor, so the gain is again 50 × 10 × 72.5 = 36,250 J.
  3. (iii) The change in potential energy depends only on the vertical height between the start and the end, not on the path taken.

(i) 36,250 J (ii) 36,250 J (iii) The gain in potential energy is the same for any path; only the height risen matters.

Q6 A crane lifts a mass m to the 10th floor of a building in a certain time. It then raises the same mass to the 20th floor of the same building in double the time. How much more energy and power are required? Assume that the height of all floors is equal.

Given

  • A crane lifts mass m to the 10th floor in a certain time t.
  • It then raises the same mass to the 20th floor in double the time, 2t.
  • All floors have equal height.

Asked

How much more energy and power the second lift needs.

How the animation is drawn

  • The question does not say where the second lift starts. Because it asks how much more energy is needed, both lifts are drawn starting from the ground. (If the second lift started from the 10th floor, it would need the same energy and half the power.)
  • Floors are counted from the ground floor at height 0, so the 10th floor is 10 floor heights up.
  • The two lifts are shown side by side so they can be compared. Each load rises at a steady speed, with a brief speed-up and slow-down.
  • The energy bars share one scale, and so do the power bars.

Answer

  1. Let each floor have height d. The 10th floor is at 10d and the 20th floor at 20d.
  2. Energy: E1 = mg × 10d = 10mgd; E2 = mg × 20d = 20mgd = 2E1. The second lift needs 10mgd more: twice the energy.
  3. Power: P1 = 10mgd/t; P2 = 20mgd/(2t) = 10mgd/t = P1. No extra power is needed.
  4. Other reading: if the second lift started from the 10th floor, it would need 10mgd (no more energy) and 10mgd/(2t) (half the power).

Twice the energy (10mgd more), but the same power.

Q7 Which factors determine the energy required to raise a flag from the ground to the top of a tall flagpole using a pulley? Does raising the flag slowly or quickly change the amount of work done? If the speed at which the flag is raised is doubled, how does the power requirement change? Explain your answers.

Given

  • A flag is raised from near the ground to the top of a flagpole with a pulley.
  • It may be raised slowly or quickly; then the speed is doubled.

Asked

The factors that decide the energy needed; whether the speed changes the work done; how doubling the speed changes the power.

How the animation is drawn

  • No values are given: the mass m, the height h and the speed v are kept as symbols.
  • The pulley is ideal (no friction). A real pulley needs a little extra work against friction.
  • Each flag moves at a steady speed, with a brief speed-up and slow-down at the ends; force arrows are shown only while the speed is steady, when the pull equals the weight.
  • The work bars share one scale, and so do the power bars. A mark on the rope shows the rope moving down as the flag rises.

Answer

  1. Energy needed = work done against gravity = gain in potential energy = mgh. It depends on the mass of the flag (m) and the height of the pole (h), and on g. A real pulley also needs a little extra energy to overcome friction.
  2. Raising the flag slowly or quickly does not change the work done: the same weight is raised through the same height.
  3. Doubling the speed halves the time. Power P = W/t = mgh/(t/2) = 2mgh/t, so the power doubles.

The energy depends on the flag’s mass and the pole’s height (and g, plus pulley friction). The speed does not change the work done. Doubling the speed doubles the power.
