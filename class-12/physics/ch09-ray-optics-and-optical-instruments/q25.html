9.25 Answer the following questions: (a) The angle subtended at the eye by an object is equal to the angle subtended at the eye by the virtual image produced by a magnifying glass. In what sense then does a magnifying glass provide angular magnification? (b) In viewing through a magnifying glass, one usually positions one’s eyes very close to the lens. Does angular magnification change if the eye is moved back? (c) Magnifying power of a simple microscope is inversely proportional to the focal length of the lens. What then stops us from using a convex lens of smaller and smaller focal length and achieving greater and greater magnifying power? (d) Why must both the objective and the eyepiece of a compound microscope have short focal lengths? (e) When viewing through a compound microscope, our eyes should be positioned not on the eyepiece but a short distance away from it for best viewing. Why? How much should be that short distance between the eye and eyepiece?

Given

  • Simple and compound microscopes.

Asked

Five explanations.

Why there is no animation

  • This question asks for explanations or algebraic deductions rather than describing one situation to follow; the page shows the question and a worked answer.

Answer

  1. (a) Without the lens the object cannot be brought closer than 25 cm and still be seen clearly. With the lens it can be much closer (about f), so it subtends a larger angle, and its image is still seen comfortably: that is the gain.
  2. (b) Yes: moving the eye back makes the image subtend a slightly smaller angle at the eye, so the magnification falls a little (and the field of view shrinks).
  3. (c) Lenses of very short focal length are hard to make and suffer badly from spherical and chromatic aberration.
  4. (d) The objective’s magnification is roughly L/fo and the eyepiece’s D/fe; both short focal lengths make both large.
  5. (e) The eye should be at the eye-ring, the image of the objective formed by the eyepiece, where all the light passing through the microscope crosses; the eye then collects all of it and sees the widest field. Its distance from the eyepiece depends on the separation of the lenses and fe (typically a few millimetres to a centimetre or so).

See the answers to (a)–(e).
